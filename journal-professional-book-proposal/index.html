Preparing a Professional Book Proposal

A proposal makes a book’s premise, readership, and execution legible before the entire work is in front of an editor.

ELIJAH EDITORIAL JOURNAL · PUBLISHING PERSPECTIVES

Define the promise of the book

Begin with the central question or idea. Explain what the book will help a reader understand, experience, or reconsider. A proposal becomes harder to assess when it tries to promise something for everyone or lists themes without an organizing argument.

Draft a short overview in plain language. Then ask a reader unfamiliar with the subject to describe the book back to you. The gaps between the two descriptions often reveal where the premise needs sharpening.

Connect audience, context, and structure

Describe a plausible readership and explain how comparable books help locate the project. Comparisons work best when they identify a specific relationship—subject, approach, form, or audience—rather than suggesting identical commercial results.

A chapter outline should show progression. Explain what each chapter contributes and why it belongs in that sequence. Sample material should demonstrate the voice and method the overview promises.

Keep supporting claims proportionate

Describe your experience and access accurately. Distinguish research already completed from work still planned, and existing reader connections from aspirations. These distinctions help a recipient evaluate both the idea and the practical path to completing it.

Proposal requirements vary by category and recipient. Confirm the requested materials before sending. Fiction is often considered through a completed manuscript and supporting documents rather than a standard nonfiction proposal.
